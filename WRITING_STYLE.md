# Author's writing style for agents

Use this guide when drafting or revising text in my voice. It describes how
I structure explanations, paragraphs and transitions. Project instructions,
the intended audience and the evidence still govern the content.

## Basis and limits

This guide is based on the author's writing sample, especially
the preface, introductions, discussions and conclusions. Use the sample for
style only. Do not cite it, import its findings or references, or copy
its sentences into another project. The guide is an editorial interpretation
of recurring patterns, not a rule to imitate every sentence.

Preserve the way I develop an argument while improving grammar and clarity.
Do not reproduce typographical errors, repeated wording or awkward English
from the sample. Do not mistake pasted material or AI drafts in project notes
for my own voice.

## How I develop an explanation

Start with the subject and why it matters. Establish enough context for the
reader to understand the problem before introducing the method or tool.
Move from the broader question to a specific difficulty, then explain the
approach and what it makes possible.

My writing often connects several kinds of knowledge rather than treating
each as an isolated topic. Explain those connections: how one step depends
on another, why different sources need to be combined, or how a technical
choice affects the wider research question.

Make the purpose of a section apparent early. Develop it through examples
and explanations, then show its implication or the question it leaves open.
Use this sequence where it fits; do not impose it on every section.

## Paragraphs and rhythm

Write connected paragraphs, each with one main idea. A paragraph will often
need four to six sentences, but its length should follow the explanation.
Avoid turning every sentence into a separate paragraph.

The opening sentence introduces the subject. The following sentences explain
it, give an example or show the relationship between its parts. The closing
sentence can explain why this matters or prepare the next paragraph.

Vary sentence length. Combine short statements with fuller explanations so
that the text reads as a developing argument. Avoid a sequence of clipped
fragments, commands and slogans. Split an overloaded sentence without losing
the connection between its parts.

Use transitions when they express a real relationship. For example:

- **For example** introduces a concrete case.
- **In addition** adds a related consideration.
- **However**, **yet** or **still** identifies a limitation or unresolved issue.
- **To address this** connects a problem to the approach taken.
- **This means** or **this makes it possible** explains a consequence.

Do not repeat the same transition at the start of every paragraph. Where
possible, carry a specific subject from one paragraph into the next instead.

## Tone and word choice

Use a thoughtful, explanatory voice. Write as a researcher explaining the
work to a colleague, with enough detail to make the reasoning visible.
Be direct about practical problems, but do not turn the explanation into
advertising, a motivational speech or a stream of warnings.

Prefer concrete subjects and verbs: people curate data, a script retrieves
records, a standard allows comparison, a method leaves a gap. Name the people,
work and decisions behind abstract terms where the information is available.

Use familiar words. Introduce necessary technical terms with an explanation
and an example when useful. Do not add jargon to make the text sound academic.
Avoid inflated adjectives, fashionable business language and formulaic phrases
such as "it's worth noting", "delve", "leverage" and "cutting-edge".

Use "I" for my own experience, choices and interpretation when the genre
allows it. Use "we" for a shared reasoning process or an actual team's work.
Do not invent personal experience or change who performed the work.

For English projects, default to British spelling and `-ise` forms unless
the project specifies otherwise. Preserve proper names and exact quotations.

## Examples, limitations and conclusions

Use examples to explain a general point. Introduce the example, show what
it demonstrates, then return to the argument. Avoid adding examples that
require unsupported facts or distract from the question.

Keep limitations alongside the result or method they qualify. Distinguish
observations, predictions, interpretations and proposals. Preserve disagreement
between sources and explain it fairly rather than smoothing it into consensus.

Bring related points together at the end of a section when useful. Explain
what follows from them and what remains to be done. Avoid ending every
section with a slogan or a generic summary.

## Structure and formatting

Use headings to mark changes in subject or purpose. Keep their wording plain.
Use lists for steps, criteria or parallel items that readers need to compare.
Use prose for explanations and connections. A list should not replace the
argument that makes its items meaningful.

Follow the project's formatting conventions. Do not add tables, callout boxes,
bold text or extra subsections merely to make the document look more structured.

## Revising existing text

Read the complete section before editing. Keep its meaning, attribution,
uncertainty and scope. Revise one section at a time in a reviewable diff.

During revision, check:

- Does the reader know what this paragraph is about and why it is here?
- Does each sentence develop the idea or explain its connection to the next?
- Are compressed phrases and fragments hiding an explanation?
- Does the example support the point being made?
- Are claims and limitations still attached to the right sources?
- Does the text sound natural when read aloud?

Do not add a claim, citation, personal judgement or stronger conclusion solely
to improve the flow. Report any substantive change separately from language edits.

## Example of the intended flow

The following is newly written to illustrate paragraph structure, not a
quotation from the author's writing sample or a source for scientific claims.

**Compressed draft:**

> Data scattered. Different formats. Integration is the bottleneck. Use standards.

**Preferred revision:**

> The data are spread across several resources, each with its own format.
> Bringing them together requires us to check how the same information is
> described in each resource. A shared standard helps with this comparison,
> but records still need to be checked before they can be combined. This work
> makes the resulting dataset easier to interpret and use.
